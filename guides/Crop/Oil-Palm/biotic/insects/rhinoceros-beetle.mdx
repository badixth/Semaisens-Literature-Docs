Rhinoceros beetle (_Oryctes rhinoceros_ in Asia and Africa, _Oryctes_ spp. and _Strategus_ in the Americas) is the number one pest of oil palm at and shortly after **replanting**. Adult beetles bore into the crown and feed on the developing spear, producing the diagnostic V-shaped cuts on newly opened fronds. Severe infestation on young palms causes stunting, crown deformity, and death. The larvae breed in decaying palm trunks, so the pest and replanting are permanently linked.

**Regional priority:** Very high in Asia and Africa (_O. rhinoceros_). Present with different species in Latin America (_Strategus_). Especially critical during the first 3 to 4 years after replanting.

## When oil palm is most vulnerable

From nursery through year 4 to 5. Damage is most severe in the first 24 months after field planting, when a single beetle attack can kill the growing point of a young palm. Mature palms tolerate damage better but still lose photosynthetic area.

## How rhinoceros beetle shows in satellite data

- **Reduced canopy cover** on young palms in high-resolution imagery - stunted, damaged crowns fail to fill the expected canopy area for their age.
- **Palm mortality gaps** in replant blocks visible as thinning stand.
- **No reliable field-scale NDVI signal** on mature palms - individual frond damage does not aggregate visibly.
- **Pheromone trap catch data** is the primary quantitative early-warning signal, not imagery.

<Warning>
  Rhino beetle damage is easiest to prevent, hardest to reverse. Beetle populations build fast in windrowed replant residues. Sanitation at replanting is more important than any trapping program applied later.
</Warning>

## Field symptoms

- **V-shaped or triangular notches** on newly opened fronds - the classic diagnostic sign. Cuts are made while the frond is still folded in the spear.
- **Bore holes** in the crown near the base of the spear.
- **Frass and chewed tissue** at the bore entry.
- **Broken or truncated spear** in severe attacks.
- **Stunted and deformed young palm crowns** on chronically attacked palms.
- **Palm death** on young palms if the growing point is destroyed.
- **Adult beetles** visible in the crown at night; larvae found in decaying trunk material.

## Reduce the damage by

- **Breeding-site sanitation at replanting** - the single most important control. Chip and spread felled trunks, or bury deeply. Do not leave large windrowed piles of decaying trunks; they become massive beetle breeding sites.
- **Cover chipped residues** with legume cover crops or mulch that speeds decomposition and denies breeding habitat.
- **Pheromone trapping** - deploy ethyl 4-methyloctanoate (aggregation pheromone) traps at recommended density during replanting and the first 3 to 4 years. Maintain regularly - dry, un-serviced traps do nothing.
- **Biological control** - release _Metarhizium anisopliae_ (fungal biocontrol) into breeding sites; use _Oryctes rhinoceros_ nudivirus (OrNV) where locally available and permitted.
- **Ground application of prophylactic insecticide** at the leaf axil of young palms in high-pressure zones - use only when scouting justifies it.
- **Regular scouting** during the vulnerable window - inspect the crown of every young palm at least monthly.
- **Coordinate with neighbors** - beetle populations move across boundaries; regional replanting synchronization and shared breeding-site management are more effective than individual response.

## Set an alert for early warning

<Tip>
  Turn the diagnosis into a trigger. Rhino beetle management is a calendar and trap-count discipline, backed by scouting during the vulnerable window.
</Tip>

Recommended settings for rhinoceros beetle on oil palm:

- **Monthly trap catch review** during the first 4 years after replanting.
- **Scouting reminder** - monthly crown inspection of every young palm on replant blocks.
- **Replanting sanitation checkpoint** - schedule a sanitation audit 3, 6, and 12 months after replant to verify residue chipping and cover-crop establishment.
- **Regional coordination alert** - flag any nearby replanting activity as a rising beetle pressure signal.
