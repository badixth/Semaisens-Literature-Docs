Copper deficiency in oil palm is nicknamed **"peat yellows"** because it is almost universal on deep peat soils and common on sandy or heavily leached mineral soils. Peat locks up copper in organic complexes; sandy soils simply have very little to start with. Symptoms show on the mid-canopy fronds, giving a distinctive stratified color pattern where the youngest fronds are green, mid fronds are yellow-chlorotic, and older fronds may still be green.

**Regional priority:** Very high on peat plantations (Sarawak, Sumatra, Kalimantan, coastal Malaysia). Common on sandy soils in West Africa. Rare on well-mineralized clay soils.

## When oil palm is most vulnerable

Any age, but Cu deficiency is a chronic condition on peat that must be managed continuously through the stand's life. Nursery and immature palms on peat show the most dramatic symptoms; mature palms show milder but persistent deficiency.

## How copper deficiency shows in satellite data

- **Block-wide NDVI decline** on chronic peat blocks compared to the [ecosystem baseline](/guides/Crop/Oil-Palm/oil-palm-ecosystems).
- **True-color imagery** may show a pale-yellow tint on the mid-canopy of affected blocks.
- Not distinguishable from Nitrogen deficiency in imagery alone - both look pale from above. Ground inspection of _which fronds_ are affected tells them apart.
- Slow trend; monthly to quarterly review is appropriate.

<Note>
  On peat, if the whole canopy looks pale and Cu treatment does not green it up, suspect Nitrogen instead. The two commonly co-deficit and are usually managed together.
</Note>

## Field symptoms

- **Mid-canopy fronds chlorotic** while youngest and oldest fronds are relatively green - the diagnostic stratified pattern.
- **Interveinal yellowing** with green veins on affected leaflets.
- **Small, narrow leaflets** on chronically deficient palms.
- **Reduced frond length and canopy area** in chronic cases.
- **Necrotic spots** on the mid-canopy leaflets in severe deficiency.
- **Slow growth** on nursery and immature palms on peat.

## Reduce the damage by

- **Copper sulphate (CuSO4) soil application** - typically 25 to 100 g per palm per year on peat, applied to the palm circle. Frequency depends on soil test and leaf analysis.
- **Foliar copper sprays** for rapid correction on symptomatic immature palms - 0.5% CuSO4 solution.
- **Copper-containing compound fertilizers** where custom mixing is not available.
- **Peat water table management** - a controlled water table (50 to 70 cm) improves Cu availability by preventing extreme reduction.
- **Do not over-lime peat** - excess Ca raises pH and further reduces Cu availability.
- **Avoid high P applications alone** - excess P can induce Cu deficiency.
- **Manage Cu together with N and other peat-specific nutrients** - peat blocks need an integrated nutrient program, not element-by-element correction.
- **Test Cu at every routine leaf sampling** on peat and sandy blocks.

<Warning>
  Copper is toxic at high rates. Foliar sprays over 0.5% or repeated soil applications without leaf-test justification can cause copper toxicity. Confirm deficiency before repeat treatments.
</Warning>

## Set an alert for early warning

<Tip>
  Turn the diagnosis into a trigger. Copper is a slow, chronic peat-block condition; use annual leaf sampling and quarterly imagery review rather than fast alerts.
</Tip>

Recommended settings for copper on oil palm:

- **Annual leaf sampling reminder** for frond 17 Cu on all peat and sandy blocks.
- **Quarterly imagery review** of peat blocks - trigger on NDVI below the peat-adjusted baseline.
- **Peat block flag** - tag all peat blocks in the field data model so they receive Cu-specific fertilizer plans.
