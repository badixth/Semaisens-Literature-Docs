Iron toxicity is common on acid sulfate soils, low-lying valley bottoms, and inland valleys with prolonged flooding. Under strong soil reduction, iron becomes soluble at concentrations that damage rice roots and leaves. It is a _toxicity_, not a deficiency, and it responds to water and pH management more than to fertilizer.

## When rice is most vulnerable

From seedling establishment through early tillering. Severe iron toxicity can kill young plants; moderate cases stunt tillering and cap final yield.

## How iron toxicity shows in satellite data

- **Low, patchy NDVI** in low-lying zones and toe-slopes where water accumulates and stays reduced.
- Same zones affected season after season, closely correlated with elevation and drainage.
- Signature overlaps with poor soil, submergence, and salinity - use soil pH, standing water depth, and the bronze leaf color on the ground to separate them.

## Field symptoms

- Tiny brown to bronze-orange stipples on lower leaves, spreading and coalescing into a bronzed appearance
- Purplish-brown streaks along the leaf blade in some cultivars
- Stunted plants with limited tillering and dark, blackened, sparse root system
- Yellow-orange discoloration in severe cases, mistaken for tungro or N deficiency
- Affected patches follow topography (low spots, water-collecting zones)

## Reduce the damage by

- **Improve drainage** - alternate wetting and drying reduces soil reduction and iron solubility.
- **Delay transplanting** by a few weeks on affected fields to let reduction subside after initial flooding.
- **Apply lime** on acid soils (pH below 5.0) to precipitate iron.
- **Use iron-toxicity-tolerant cultivars** such as those released from Africa Rice and IRRI screening for iron-toxic environments.
- **Balance nutrients** - adequate P, K, and Zn reduce the severity of iron toxicity symptoms.
- **Do not confuse with brown spot or tungro** - confirm with soil pH and water history.
